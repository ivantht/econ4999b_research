## ABSTRACT

This paper examines the effects of country-specific geopolitical risk on capital flow volatility and asset markets across 29 emerging and advanced economies over the period 2000–2023. Using panel regressions and a panel structural vector autoregression framework, the results show that geopolitical risk raises bond yields and leads to exchange rate depreciation, with stronger and more persistent effects in emerging economies. Asset markets for advanced economies are affected mainly through lower equity prices. The impact on capital flow volatility is slightly higher on average for advanced economies but remains more persistent for emerging economies. Greater financial development, higher central bank independence, and lower public debt mitigate the adverse effects of geopolitical risk on both capital flows and asset markets. These findings highlight the importance of strong macroeconomic fundamentals and institutional frameworks in building resilience against geopolitical shocks.

Keywords: geopolitical risk, capital flow volatility, financial markets

JEL codes: G15, G41

## I. INTRODUCTION

Geopolitical risk has emerged as a major factor affecting global financial markets and global economic prospects. Episodes of amplified geopolitical tensions can trigger rises in global risk aversion and spikes in oil prices, with spillovers to asset prices and the trajectory of capital flows. Recent work in this growing literature has highlighted that the key channels through which geopolitical risk affects financial markets are investor sentiment (e.g., Hachicha 2023, Banerjee, Kumar, and Goodell 2024) and market liquidity (e.g., Abdel-Latif and El-Gamal 2020), with emerging markets particularly affected (e.g., Das, Kannadhasan, and Bhattacharyya 2019; Mansour-Ichrakieh and Zeaiter 2019).

While the literature has tended to focus on the macroeconomic consequences of geopolitical risks, there has been less work examining the spillover effects of geopolitical risks on capital flow volatility and asset markets, especially from the comparative perspective of emerging and advanced economies. A few recent studies have explored the negative impact of global geopolitical risk on global financial markets (Baur and Smales 2020, Smales 2021, Izzeldin et al. 2023, Yilmazkuday 2024). However, there is less direct empirical evidence on the effects of country-specific geopolitical risks on domestic financial markets and capital flow volatility. Against this backdrop, this paper employs a set of panel analyses, as well as a panel structural vector autoregression (VAR) model, to estimate the impacts of geopolitical risk on domestic asset markets and capital flow volatility in 29 emerging and advanced economies over the period 2000 to 2023.

To measure the country-specific geopolitical risks, we directly use the monthly country-specific geopolitical risk index (GPR index) constructed by Caldara and Iacoviello (2022), which offers two advantages for studying the relationship between geopolitical risk and financial markets. First, unlike data that would inform only sporadic geopolitical events, the GPR index provides monthly data dating back to 1985, offering a long time series that allows for an in-depth analysis of capital flow and asset price dynamics in response to GPR fluctuations. Second, unlike many other uncertainty proxies, the GPR index does not consistently rise during recessions or financial crises but spikes in response to events like wars or terrorist attacks. This makes the GPR index particularly useful for isolating the effects of geopolitical risk on financial markets posed by other forms of uncertainty and for addressing endogeneity concerns.

Our analyses consist of two stages. The first stage uses a panel regression framework to examine the role of geopolitical risks in affecting capital flow volatility and asset markets, controlling for both domestic fundamentals and global factors. In the case of emerging economies, geopolitical risk has a significant positive effect on bond yields, while equities significantly decline and these economies also incur significant currency depreciation. The effects on bond yields and currencies are stronger for emerging than for advanced economies. However, for equity prices, the impact is marginally higher in magnitude in the case of advanced economies. In addition, as regards capital flow volatility, we find that the average impact is slightly higher for advanced economies.

To explore whether the heterogeneity in country-specific characteristics across countries could affect domestic asset market responses to geopolitical risks, we estimate the panel regressions’ conditioning on financial development, central bank independence, and public debt levels. These conditioning factors are shown to have a moderating effect on the impact of geopolitical risks on asset markets and capital flow volatility. Countries with higher financial development exhibit weaker responses in stock prices, bond yields, and exchange rates, though they remain more vulnerable to capital flow volatility owing to their greater global financial integration. Conversely, economies with lower financial development experience more severe depreciation in exchange rates. Similarly, countries with greater central bank independence show resilience to geopolitical risks, with the adverse effects on domestic asset markets yielding insignificant results. In contrast, countries with lower central bank independence face sharper declines in stock prices and exchange rates alongside rising bond yields and capital flow volatility. Public debt also plays a critical role, with lower-debt countries experiencing milder financial market disruptions, while high-debt economies face amplified adverse effects.

Finally, we investigate the dynamic effects of geopolitical risks on capital flow volatility and financial market variables using a panel structural VAR (SVAR) model. This approach, which also controls for domestic fundamentals and global factors identified in the baseline panel ordinary least squares (OLS) model, allows us to capture both the duration and the dynamics of these effects over time. These dynamic results provide further evidence that heightened geopolitical risks significantly increase capital flow volatility and long-term bond yields, exacerbating financial instability. Geopolitical risk shocks also exert significant and persistent negative effects on stock prices and exchange rates, indicating that rising geopolitical risks weaken both equity and currency markets. In the case of emerging economies, the response of capital flow volatility to geopolitical risk shocks is higher in magnitude at peak than that of advanced economies, while the response also remains statistically significant for a longer duration (for around 6 months, compared to 3 months for advanced economies). The dynamic analysis also reveals a stronger worsening reaction in bond yields, stock prices, and currencies in the case of emerging compared to advanced economies.

Overall, we find that heightened geopolitical risks disrupt domestic asset markets and increase capital flow volatility. The severity of these adverse effects varies depending on country-specific characteristics. Our findings suggest that greater central bank independence and lower debt can help mitigate the impact of geopolitical risks on asset markets and capital flow volatility. Enhancing financial development can also help moderate the effects of geopolitical risks on asset markets. However, effective macroprudential policy and capital flow management frameworks are key aspects for consideration in helping mitigate capital flow volatility. In addition, given that rising geopolitical risks can create a more challenging environment for central banks to implement effective monetary policies, this may have implications for monetary policy operational frameworks. Policymakers should also strengthen coordination between monetary and macroprudential policies, which can help stabilize markets during episodes of elevated geopolitical risk.

This paper contributes to the growing literature on the financial market implications of geopolitical risk. The existing literature on the financial market implications of geopolitical risks tends to focus on particular asset market segments, particularly the stock market and commodities. Balcilar et al. (2018) find that geopolitical risk largely affects stock market volatility rather than returns across the BRICS economies.[^1] Similar effects are found by Salisu et al. (2022) and Zhang et al. (2023) for other sets of economies. Hoque and Zaidi (2020) note that significant nonlinearity prevails in the relationship between geopolitical risk and emerging stock markets.

Some other studies have focused on the effects on particular sectors, including defense, tourism, and renewable energy (e.g., Apergis et al. 2018, Demiralay and Kilincarslan 2019, Jiang et al. 2020). On commodities, several studies have examined the role of geopolitical risk in amplifying commodity price volatilities in respect of oil (e.g., Liu et al. 2019; Mei et al. 2020; Qin et al. 2020; Wang, Su, and Umar 2021; Behera, Priyadarsini, and Patnaik 2024) and precious metals (e.g., Banerjee, Ghosal, and Mukherjee 2019; Gkillas, Gupta, and Pierdzioch 2020).

Some recent work has explored its implications for currencies and capital flows. Ma (2025) finds that heightened geopolitical risk leads to currency depreciation in Asia, while Zhang and Chen (2025) show that rising economic uncertainty increases capital flow volatility.

However, the broader question of how geopolitical risk affects capital flow volatility and asset market returns across both emerging and advanced economies remains largely unexplored. Moreover, the literature offers limited insights into the role of domestic fundamentals in mitigating such spillovers.

This paper fills these gaps in three ways. First, we provide a comprehensive cross-country analysis of the effects of geopolitical risk on both capital flow volatility and three asset market returns across 29 advanced and emerging economies from 2000 to 2023. Second, we identify the role of domestic fundamentals (notably financial development, central bank independence, and public debt levels) in mitigating the extent of geopolitical risk spillovers. Third, by using both panel regression and panel SVAR approaches, we capture not only the average effects of geopolitical risk but also its dynamic propagation over time.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section II describes the data and outlines the empirical methodology. Section III presents the empirical results. Section IV concludes.

## II. DATA AND EMPIRICAL METHODOLOGY

In this section, we first discuss the measure of geopolitical risk. We then describe the data source for variables we use in the empirical analysis. Finally, we present our econometric framework used to produce the empirical results.

### A. Country-Specific Measures of Geopolitical Risk

Geopolitical risk (GPR) is defined as the uncertainty arising from wars, terrorist acts, and interstate tensions that disrupt the normal functioning of international relations. In this paper, we use the monthly country-specific GPR index constructed by Caldara and Iacoviello (2022), which follows the methodological frameworks developed by Saiz and Simonsohn (2013) and Baker, Bloom, and Davies (2016). The index is based on the frequency of articles referencing geopolitical events and risks in 11 major English-language newspapers: The Boston Globe, The Chicago Tribune, The Daily Telegraph, Financial Times, The Globe and Mail, The Guardian, Los Angeles Times, The New York Times, The Times, The Wall Street Journal, and The Washington Post. The GPR index is computed as the ratio of geopolitical articles to total articles.[^2] Figure 1 plots country-specific GPR indices for our sample countries.

### B. Data

We use available monthly data with an unbalanced panel for 29 countries, covering both emerging market economies and advanced economies, spanning 2000:M1 to 2023:M12.[^3] We collect data on the real stock price index and 10-year government bond yield as indicators of the domestic financial market. For the exchange rate, we use the real effective exchange rate index. The data are from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Main Economic Indicators, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) International Financial Statistics, and the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) database.

For capital flows, we utilize the OECD Monthly Capital Flow Dataset (de Crescenzio and Lepers 2024), which offers several advantages over private data providers such as Emerging Portfolio Fund Research (EPFR) and the Institute of International Finance (IIF). First, the OECD dataset provides more comprehensive country coverage, including both emerging market economies and advanced economies, offering a more global perspective on capital flows. Second, it includes a wider range of financial transactions, such as bank flows and foreign direct investment (FDI), and captures both inflows and outflows, facilitating a comprehensive analysis of the financial account. Third, the dataset is aligned with balance of payments (BoP) principles, enhancing its reliability for cross-country economic comparisons. Fourth, the OECD dataset is publicly available, reducing dependency on costly private data sources, and is updated monthly, providing timely insights consistent with BoP standards. Specifically, we focus on portfolio inflows, which constitute a significant portion of the dataset, enabling us to extend the time series for a broader range of countries. To measure capital flow volatility, we calculate the standard deviation of capital flows using a 3-month rolling window of monthly data.[^4]

To assess the impact of geopolitical risk on capital flow volatility and financial markets, we incorporate several control variables for both domestic fundamentals and global factors, based on the related literature. For domestic macroeconomic fundamentals, we include the real industrial production index as a measure of domestic output, year-on-year changes in the consumer price index as an indicator of inflation, and central bank policy rates. These data are sourced from the IMF’s International Financial Statistics and the BIS database. For global factors, we use data from various sources: the West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil price as a proxy for global oil prices, the VIX index (the Chicago Board Options Exchange [CBOE] Volatility Index) to capture global financial market uncertainty, and the Economic Policy Uncertainty (EPU) index from Baker, Bloom, and Davies (2016) to measure global economic policy uncertainty.

Recognizing the importance of structural country-specific characteristics, we include several additional variables in our empirical analysis. To gauge the level of financial development, we use the IMF’s Financial Development Index, which assesses the depth, access, and efficiency of financial institutions and markets. We also incorporate the Central Bank Independence Index from Romelli (2022) as a measure of the degree of central bank independence. Finally, we include the government debt-to-gross domestic product (GDP) ratio, sourced from the IMF’s World Economic Outlook, to capture public indebtedness.

### C. Econometric Methodology

Our econometric methodology is underpinned by international portfolio choice and asset pricing theories, augmented with a geopolitical risk component. Theory indicates that higher uncertainty driven by elevated geopolitical risk triggers capital flow volatility as investors rebalance toward safe havens. Meanwhile, equity returns decline, sovereign risk premia rise, and currencies face depreciating pressures. More specifically, our empirical framework is founded on a generalized capital asset pricing model (Sharpe 1964) of the following form:

$$
E(R)_i = R_f + \beta_i[E(R)_m - R_f] + \lambda_i(Risk_i) \qquad (1)
$$

where $E(R)_i$ is the expected return on asset $i$, $R_f$ is the risk-free rate, $\beta_i$ is the asset's exposure to the global markets, and $\lambda_i$ denotes exposure to country-specific risk factors, including geopolitical risk.

Based on the Capital Asset Pricing Model framework, priors are informed on how heightened geopolitical risk can be expected to affect capital flow volatility and asset markets. For example, where geopolitical risk rises, the required return on assets would rise in order to compensate investors. This would trigger an outward reallocation of capital and amplified capital flow volatility. The same underlying premise applies to equity prices, bond yields, and exchange rates. More specifically, higher geopolitical risk leads to a higher risk premium on assets and a higher required return for holding that asset, triggering reallocation to less risky assets. Higher required returns on equities imply lower equity prices, higher required returns on bonds imply lower bond prices (or higher yields to compensate for the higher risk of default), and currencies depreciate as a higher risk premium leads to safe-haven flows of capital out of risky assets.

Turning to the methodology, the first stage examines the role of geopolitical risks in affecting capital flow volatility and domestic asset markets, controlling for both domestic fundamentals and global factors. Specifically, the following baseline equation is estimated:

$$
y_{i,t}^{j} = \beta GPR_{i,t} + \gamma X_{i,t-1} + GEPU_{t-1} + VIX_{t-1} + \delta_i + \varepsilon_{i,t} \qquad (2)
$$

where $i = 1, \cdots N$ refers to the specific economy in the sample; $y$ is the variable of interest, including capital flow volatility, the log of stock prices, long-term bond yield, and the log of the real effective exchange rate; $GPR$ is the series of the GPR index (standardized by country); and $X$ is a vector of domestic fundamentals, including the log of real industrial production, inflation, and the policy rate, along with country-specific characteristics such as financial development, central bank independence, and the public debt-to-GDP ratio. $GEPU$ and $VIX$ represent the log of the global EPU index and the log of the VIX index, respectively, to account for global factors. $\delta_i$ denotes economy-specific fixed effects, controlling for the time-invariant characteristics of the country. Finally, $\varepsilon_{i,t}$ denotes error term. Control variables are lagged by one period to mitigate potential endogeneity concerns.

To examine whether heterogeneity in country-specific characteristics influences the effect of geopolitical risk, we divide the sample into groups based on whether they are emerging or advanced economies, their levels of financial development, central bank independence, and public indebtedness, and estimate separate regressions for each group.

We further examine the dynamic responses of capital flow volatility and financial market variables to geopolitical risk shocks using a panel SVAR model. The analysis also controls for the domestic fundamentals and global factors outlined in the baseline OLS model. This approach enables us to identify the dynamics and duration of the effect of geopolitical risks. The panel SVAR can be denoted as follows in its general specification, with structural shocks identified by a recursive restriction:

$$
Y_{i,t} = A(L)Y_{i,t-1} + \alpha_i + \mu_{i,t} \qquad (3)
$$

where $Y_{i,t}$ refers to a vector of our selected endogenous variables of country $i$, including the GPR index, capital flow volatility, the logarithm of stock prices, the long-term bond yield, and the logarithm of the real effective exchange rate. Additionally, global factors are represented by the logarithm of the global EPU index and the logarithm of the VIX index, while domestic fundamentals include the logarithm of real GDP, the inflation rate, and the policy rate; $A(L)$ is a matrix of polynomials in the lag operator $L$; $\alpha_i$ denotes country-specific fixed effects to account for unobserved time-invariant heterogeneity across countries; and $\mu_t$ is a vector of disturbances. Given the autoregressive nature of the panel SVAR model, fixed effects are intrinsically correlated with the regressors. Hence, we use the forward orthogonal deviation procedure to eliminate fixed effects, such that the transformed variables are orthogonal to the lagged regressors. The lag structure in the panel SVAR (three lags) has been selected using the Schwarz information criterion (SIC).

To isolate the impact of geopolitical risk from the current or past performance of domestic fundamentals and global factors, we adopt a recursive identification scheme as outlined by Christiano, Eichenbaum, and Evans (1999). In this framework, variables ordered higher in the sequence are insulated from contemporaneous shocks originating from those ordered below them, while lower-ranked variables are influenced by contemporaneous shocks from those above. Specifically, we position the GPR index at the top of the ordering, ensuring it is influenced only by its own contemporaneous shocks.[^5] Following this, we place the global factor variables, meaning they are affected by both their own shocks and those from the GPR index, but remain unaffected by any contemporaneous shocks from variables ordered below them. Next, we order the domestic macroeconomic fundamentals, ensuring they are placed before the capital flow volatility and financial market variables. Importantly, we position capital flow volatility and financial market variables at the bottom of the ordering.

This ordering scheme is based not only on the assumption that these variables are influenced by the GPR index, global factors, and domestic fundamentals but also on our first-stage empirical results, which indicate that capital flow volatility and financial markets are driven by both push and pull factors. The resulting relationships between the variables within the SVAR setup are therefore manifested in the impulse response functions. As a robustness test on the impulse response functions, we also examine an alternative ordering scheme in the SVAR.

Our model accounts for a variety of domestic and global factors, the effects of which align with the related literature. ### B. Heterogeneity Analyses

In this part of the analysis, we explore how the impact of geopolitical risks on capital flow volatility and asset markets varies based on country-specific characteristics. Specifically, we examine regional differences (i.e., emerging versus advanced economies), the effects conditional on the extent of financial development, central bank independence, and public debt levels. By comparing these factors across countries, we gain insights into how different structural and macroeconomic environments influence the transmission of geopolitical shocks into financial markets.

#### 1. Emerging Versus Advanced Economies

We further disaggregate our sample into emerging and advanced economies to explore regional heterogeneity in the effects of geopolitical risks. Table 3 presents the estimated impact of these risks on capital flow volatility, stock prices, bond yields, and exchange rates across these regions. The results underscore notable differences between the two groups. For emerging economies, the effects of heightened geopolitical risks on bond yields and exchange rates are particularly pronounced when these countries are compared to advanced economies. In contrast, advanced economies tend to experience more significant disruptions in stock prices and capital flow volatility on average in response to rising geopolitical risks. Regional factors and underlying fundamentals may therefore play a critical role in shaping the transmission of geopolitical shocks to domestic financial markets. These findings motivate further granularity in the empirical exercise, including exploring the role of country-specific factors affecting the overall findings, as well as dynamic analysis, as described earlier.

### C. Dynamic Effects of Geopolitical Risks

Finally, we investigate the dynamic effects of geopolitical risks on capital flow volatility and financial market variables, using the panel SVAR model outlined in Section II.C. Figure 2 shows the estimated impulse responses based on the panel SVAR model. The solid line in each graph represents the estimated impulse responses over the following 9 months to a positive geopolitical risk shock—namely, a sudden elevation in geopolitical risk. Specifically, a geopolitical risk shock is defined as being 1 standard deviation from the GPR index. The grey dashed lines represent 95% confidence intervals generated by 500 Monte Carlo repetitions.

The results provide further evidence that heightened geopolitical risks significantly increase capital flow volatility and long-term bond yields, while stock prices fall. Currencies, meanwhile, are found to depreciate as a result of positive GPR shocks. More specifically, a 1 standard deviation rise in GPR leads to a more than proportional positive response in capital flow volatility at around 7 standard deviations after 2 months, with the response becoming insignificant after 3 months. For bond yields, these rise by around 0.1 percentage points at 3 months after the shock and rise gradually over the horizon, reaching 0.2 percentage points at 9 months after the shock. Significant negative responses are also found for stock prices, falling in the range of around 1–1.5% as a result of a 1 standard deviation positive GPR shock, with the maximum impact occurring in the range of 6 to 9 months after the shock.[^6] Meanwhile, currencies depreciate by around 0.4% on average during this window.

On capital flow volatility responses to GPR shocks, it is notable that, although the peak effect is marginally higher for advanced economies, the duration of the effect remains statistically significant for longer in the case of emerging economies (i.e., for around 6 months, which is twice as long as for advanced economies). For stock prices, while the impact of GPR on average (based on the panel OLS) had a marginally stronger effect for advanced economies, dynamic responses to GPR shocks reveal stronger negative impacts for emerging economies. Dynamic responses in bond yields are also found to be more pronounced for emerging economies. In addition, positive GPR shocks have stronger depreciating impacts on emerging market currencies compared to advanced economies, with a peak impact during the 6- to 9-month horizon after the GPR shock.

## IV. CONCLUSIONS

This paper provides comprehensive insights into the significant impacts of geopolitical risks on domestic financial markets and capital flow volatility across 29 emerging and advanced economies over the period 2000 to 2023. By employing a two-stage approach that combines panel regressions and a panel SVAR model, we capture both the average and the dynamic effects of geopolitical risks on financial variables, while controlling for domestic fundamentals and global factors. For emerging economies, we find that geopolitical risks have stronger impacts on raising bond yields and greater currency depreciation effects compared to advanced economies. These findings also hold in dynamic analysis. In addition, while equities in advanced economies are slightly more negatively affected on average than emerging economies, it is also important to consider the more pronounced dynamic response in emerging equities to GPR shocks. On capital flow volatility, the average impact is slightly higher in the case of advanced economies. However, subsequent dynamic analysis reveals that the impact on capital flow volatility is much more persistent in the case of emerging economies.

Furthermore, empirical findings from the heterogeneity analysis demonstrate that country-specific characteristics, such as financial development, central bank independence, and public debt levels, play critical roles in moderating these effects. More specifically, economies with stronger financial systems and greater central bank independence are better equipped to buffer against the adverse impacts of geopolitical risks, experiencing less severe disruptions in financial markets. Conversely, countries with higher public debt or lower levels of financial development face exacerbated vulnerabilities, including sharper declines in stock prices and exchange rates, as well as increased capital flow volatility. These results underline the importance of robust macroeconomic and institutional frameworks, and financial sector structures, in mitigating the adverse effects of geopolitical risks on economic stability. This study emphasizes that, while geopolitical risks pose significant challenges to both advanced and emerging economies, ability to manage these risks is strongly influenced by the strength of a country’s financial and macroeconomic fundamentals, and institutional structure. While enhancing financial development can help moderate the effects of geopolitical risks on asset markets, effective macroprudential policy and capital flow management frameworks are key aspects for consideration in helping mitigate capital flow volatility.

Geopolitical risk spillovers to domestic financial markets carry significant implications for monetary policy and central banks, especially as they heighten economic uncertainty and erode investor and consumer confidence. Such dynamics can influence inflation trajectories over the forecast horizon, complicating monetary policy decisions and potentially weakening the effectiveness of policy transmission mechanisms. As well as strengthening central bank independence, geopolitical risks also warrant consideration by central banks and financial supervisors from a financial stability perspective. Notably, our findings underscore the importance for central banks of closely monitoring how geopolitical risks spill over into asset markets. These findings are especially relevant for policymakers looking to strengthen the resilience of domestic financial markets and capital flows to external shocks driven by rising geopolitical tensions and associated economic uncertainties. Our results offer valuable insights into how monetary and macroprudential policies could be leveraged to mitigate these risks. In addition, policymakers should strengthen the coordination between monetary and macroprudential policies, which can bolster the response of monetary and financial authorities with regard to stabilizing markets during episodes of elevated geopolitical risk.

**Geopolitical Risk, Capital Flow Volatility, and Asset Market Spillovers**

This paper explores how country-specific geopolitical risks affect capital flow volatility and asset markets across 29 advanced and emerging economies from 2000 to 2023. While these risks have negative spillovers across all economies, emerging economies are especially impacted through bond yields and exchange rates. The paper also shows that deeper financial development, higher central bank independence, and lower public debt can help to mitigate the effects of geopolitical risks and strengthen resilience.
